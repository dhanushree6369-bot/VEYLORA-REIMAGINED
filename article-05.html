The Art of Listening

Sometimes, the most meaningful thing we can offer someone is our attention. But are we really listening?

Have you ever told someone something important, only to realise that they were waiting for their turn to speak?

Maybe they interrupted you, changed the subject or immediately started giving advice. You might have heard their response, but did you feel understood?

Communication is not only about expressing ourselves. It is also about making space for other people to express themselves. And that begins with listening.

Hearing and listening are not the same

Hearing is the physical process of detecting sounds. Listening involves paying attention to those sounds and trying to understand their meaning.

Imagine a friend telling you about something that happened during their day. You might hear every word while thinking about your next class, checking your phone or planning what you will say next.

You received the sounds, but you may not have fully engaged with what your friend was communicating.

What is active listening?

Active listening is an approach to communication in which we make a conscious effort to understand what another person is saying.

It involves more than remaining silent. We pay attention, respond thoughtfully and show the speaker that their message matters.

Give your attention

When possible, reduce distractions and focus on the person speaking. Small actions, such as putting your phone aside, can help show that you are present in the conversation.

Let people finish

Interrupting can make it difficult for someone to explain their thoughts. Giving them time to finish allows you to understand the complete message before responding.

Ask thoughtful questions

Questions such as "What happened next?" or "How did you feel about that?" can help someone explain their experience more clearly.

Reflect on what you heard

Sometimes it helps to restate the main idea in your own words. For example, saying "So you felt left out when that happened?" gives the other person an opportunity to clarify whether you understood them.

Listening is more than words

Communication also includes non-verbal signals. Facial expressions, posture, gestures and tone of voice can all contribute to how a message is understood.

For example, someone might say that they are fine while speaking very quietly. That does not automatically tell us how they feel, but it can remind us to approach the conversation with care rather than making assumptions.

It is also important to remember that people communicate differently. Eye contact, gestures and expressions vary between individuals and cultures. Listening should not be reduced to a checklist of body language signals.

Why do we sometimes struggle to listen?

We might be distracted, tired or thinking about something else. Sometimes we become so focused on defending our opinions that we stop trying to understand another perspective.

We may also feel pressure to find the perfect response. But not every conversation needs an immediate solution. Sometimes people simply want the space to explain what they are experiencing.

Listening without immediately fixing everything

Imagine a friend telling you that they had a difficult day. Your first instinct might be to offer advice or suggest ways to solve the problem.

Advice can be helpful, but it is worth understanding what the person needs first.

You could ask, "Would you like me to help you think through it, or would you rather I just listen?"

This simple question gives the other person a choice and helps avoid assuming what they need.

Building better conversations

  • Listen to understand rather than simply preparing a reply.
  • Allow people to express their thoughts without rushing them.
  • Ask questions when something is unclear.
  • Respect differences in opinions and experiences.
  • Remember that listening does not require agreeing with everything.

A thought to take with you

Being a good listener does not mean having all the answers. It means being willing to understand another person's perspective, even when it differs from your own.

A conversation becomes more meaningful when everyone has room to speak and feels that they have been heard.

Pause and reflect

Think about your most recent conversation with someone. Were you listening to understand, or were you mostly thinking about what to say next?

A little more understanding. A lot more connection.
