The Stories We Tell Ourselves

The little things we believe about ourselves can shape how we see the world. But are all those stories actually true?

"I'm just not good at this." "Everyone else understands it except me." "I always mess things up."

Have you ever caught yourself thinking something like this? We all have an inner voice that comments on our experiences. Sometimes it encourages us, sometimes it questions us, and sometimes it makes situations feel much bigger than they really are.

These thoughts can become stories we tell ourselves. They influence how we understand our experiences, respond to challenges and view our own abilities.

What is self-talk?

Self-talk is the internal conversation we have with ourselves. It can happen consciously, like thinking through a decision, or automatically, like having an immediate reaction after making a mistake.

For example, imagine getting a lower mark than you expected. One thought might be, "I'm terrible at this subject." Another might be, "I didn't understand this chapter yet, so I need to approach it differently."

Both thoughts respond to the same event, but they create very different ways of looking at it.

How do our experiences shape our stories?

Our beliefs about ourselves don't appear out of nowhere. They can be influenced by past experiences, feedback from other people, comparisons, expectations and the environments we grow up in.

If someone has struggled with public speaking before, they might begin to believe they are simply bad at it. Over time, that belief may make them avoid opportunities to speak, leaving them with fewer chances to discover that they can improve.

One experience can influence our expectations, but it does not have to define every experience that follows.

When thoughts become assumptions

Sometimes our minds make quick interpretations without having all the information. These are often called cognitive distortions: patterns of thinking that can make situations seem more negative or absolute than the evidence supports.

All-or-nothing thinking

Seeing something as either a complete success or a complete failure. For example, believing that one mistake means an entire presentation was terrible.

Mind reading

Assuming we know what someone else is thinking without actually asking them. For example, deciding that a friend is annoyed with us simply because they replied late.

Overgeneralisation

Taking one experience and treating it as a rule for everything. For example, thinking that because one attempt didn't work, nothing will ever work.

Can we change the story?

We don't have to accept every thought as a fact. Learning to pause and question our assumptions can help us develop a more balanced perspective.

Try asking yourself:

  • What actually happened, rather than what am I assuming?
  • Is there another way to understand this situation?
  • Would I say the same thing to a friend in this position?
  • Am I treating one difficult moment as if it defines everything?

This doesn't mean pretending that everything is positive. It means recognising difficulties without allowing one moment to become the entire story.

Understanding ourselves with a little more kindness

Self-understanding is not about constantly analysing every thought. It is about becoming curious about our reactions and recognising that we are always learning.

We can acknowledge what went wrong, take responsibility where needed and still leave room for growth. A mistake can be something we learn from rather than a label we give ourselves.

A thought to take with you

The stories we tell ourselves matter, but they are not permanent definitions of who we are. We can revisit them, question them and make space for a more balanced understanding of ourselves.

Pause and reflect

Think of a time you made an assumption about yourself. Looking back, was that assumption completely fair? What else could the situation have meant?

A little more understanding. A lot more connection.
