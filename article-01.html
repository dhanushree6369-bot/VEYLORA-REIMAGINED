Why Do We Remember Some Things and Forget Others?

From remembering an old conversation to forgetting why we entered a room, our memory is more interesting than we think.

Have you ever remembered a tiny detail from years ago but completely forgotten what you studied yesterday? Or remembered the words of a song after hearing it only a few times?

Memory is something we use every day, often without even noticing it. It helps us learn, recognise people, understand experiences and make decisions. But memory is not a perfect recording of everything that happens to us. It is an active process, and that is what makes it so fascinating.

How does memory actually work?

Psychologists often explain memory through three important processes: encoding, storage and retrieval.

1. Encoding — taking information in

Encoding is the process of turning information into a form that our brain can use. We might notice something through sight, sound or experience. Paying attention can help us encode information more effectively.

2. Storage — keeping information

Storage refers to maintaining information over time. Some information stays available only briefly, while other information can remain with us for years.

3. Retrieval — bringing information back

Retrieval happens when we access information we have previously learned. Sometimes a familiar smell, place or song can act as a reminder and help us recall an experience.

Why do certain memories stay?

Not every experience receives the same amount of attention. Information that feels meaningful, emotionally important or connected to something we already know may be easier to remember.

Think about your first day at a new school, a funny conversation with a friend or a performance you worked hard for. These experiences may stand out because they involved attention, personal meaning or strong feelings.

So, why do we forget?

Forgetting is a normal part of memory. We receive far more information every day than we can keep track of. Sometimes information becomes harder to retrieve, and sometimes it was never properly encoded because our attention was somewhere else.

For example, if you are reading a page while thinking about something completely different, you might reach the end without remembering much of what you read. You saw the words, but you may not have paid enough attention to process them.

Memory is not a perfect recording

One surprising thing about memory is that remembering is not always like replaying a video. Our recollections can be influenced by our knowledge, expectations and information we encounter later.

This is why two people can experience the same event but remember different details. It does not automatically mean that either person is deliberately making things up. Human memory is reconstructive, and our perspective can shape what we recall.

Can we support our memory?

A few everyday habits can make learning and remembering easier:

  • Give information your attention instead of multitasking.
  • Connect new ideas to things you already understand.
  • Revise information over time rather than relying on one long session.
  • Try recalling what you learned instead of only rereading it.
  • Get enough rest and give yourself breaks.

A thought to take with you

Forgetting does not mean that your memory is broken. Memory is a complex, changing process that helps us make sense of the world. Understanding how it works can help us become more thoughtful learners and more careful listeners.

Pause and reflect

What is one small memory you can recall clearly? What do you think made that particular experience memorable to you?
